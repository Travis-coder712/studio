The one rule for report-back

Key learnings, not a summary

We don't want “here's what we looked at.” We want the one or two things you now believe that you didn't before — the key learning, the number that changed your mind, and the surprise. The discussion prompts in each topic are built to push you to a position, so use them.

This is the running kit for a table-based team day on how the NEM actually evolves. The design principle is simple: put real data in front of four to eight people, give them a genuine question to argue about, and have them report a position back to the room. Everything to run it is in these tabs — one per topic, each with presenter notes.

Why it's built this way
Real data
Every figure comes from the AusEnergy “Lens” — a live database over the AEMO dispatch decade (2015→2026) plus ISP 2026, Open Electricity and CER. People argue harder about a real number than a slide. The point is discovery, not delivery.
Feel, not facts
Each topic is built around one counter-intuitive thesis a table should feel — “the last 20% costs more than the first 80%”, “demand's been flat for a decade and is about to double”. The data is there to make them believe it, or fight it.
Different lenses
Where tables share a topic, give them different questions — the weather lens vs the machines lens, find-the-knee vs wind-vs-solar. The report-back becomes a debate, not a repeat.
Built for developers
Topics 1–3 are strong market-education. Topics 4–5 speak to the job — the economics of building (value erodes as you succeed) and why so little of the pipeline gets built. Run any three to five.
The report-back — keep it sharp

Ask every table for the same three things

The call — your answer, a position not a summary.  The number — the single figure that convinced you.  The surprise — the one “I didn't realise that” moment. Optional closer: a room-wide vote on the big provocations — “Is 100% the right target?”, “Would you build another unfirmed solar farm?”, “Heatwave, broken boiler, or data-centre boom — what's the biggest risk to the grid?”

A note on the numbers

What's solid, what to check

Dispatch, generation-mix, curtailment, DER and value figures are measured and reliable. The demand-outlook absolute totals are ISP 2026 scenario figures — quote the trajectory (~60% growth, up to a rough doubling, by 2050) rather than the exact TWh, and verify absolutes against the Lens's demand-outlook view before printing. The two calculators are transparent teaching models, not planning tools — assumptions are on screen and the point is the shape of the answer.

An idea to explore — self-selecting groups by QR vote

Let the teams pick their topic from a teaser

Before the session (the day before, or as they walk in), show each person a short teaser for the five topics and let them vote by phone for the one they want — via a QR code — with a live tally so we can steer the balance: nudge people off a crowded topic, rescue an ignored one. The teaser doubles as the hook that makes the topic irresistible.

Market cyclesIn 2022 wholesale hit $205/MWh — 5× 2020. Where are we in the cycle, and what does a $10/MWh swing do to a project's returns?
RenewablesTo firm 90% wind+solar you need ~800 big batteries; 100% is a wall. How much gas keeps it sane?
DemandRooftop quietly hid ~16 TWh of demand growth. Can it keep pace with EVs and data centres — or must the grid finally grow?
EconomicsSolar's captured value fell 1.06× → 0.37× the pool — it did this to itself. Is the trough permanent, or does coal-exit bring it back?
PipelineOf 105 GW “proposed” in 2022, only ~9 GW got built. Which signals separate the real projects from the mirage?

Effort & options

  • Zero-build (best for a first run): a live poll in Slido / Mentimeter / a Google Form — QR to the poll, live bar chart of picks. ~15 min to set up, no code; the only downside is a second tool on screen.
  • Custom & on-brand (~half a day): a small Cloudflare Worker + KV — one page with the five teasers, a vote button, and a live distribution that greys out a topic once it's “full” and flags any with no takers. You already run Cloudflare, so it's free to host and sits right next to this kit.
  • In-kit note: this page is static (GitHub Pages), so a cross-phone live tally needs a tiny backend either way — the Worker is the clean version if this becomes a recurring format.

The balance mechanic: show the running count under each topic; when one hits, say, 8 people, mark it “full — pick another”, and highlight any topic still on zero. The vote becomes self-organising, evenly-sized tables — and the teasers seed the curiosity before anyone's even sat down.

Topic 1 Market cycles & shocks

The NEM is shaped by the shocks it didn't see coming

Weather and broken machines swing this market harder than any plan. Hazelwood, Callide, the 2022 suspension, Liddell — each redrew the map. You'd never have closed Liddell if you'd known what was coming. Where does today sit against the norm — a calm, well-supplied moment, or one heatwave and one broken boiler from the next one?

◆ The headline — a decade of prices, and what it does to returns

Wholesale prices move in shock-driven cycles. On the chart the swings can look gentle — but a generator's revenue is price × volume off a fixed capex base, so a small $/MWh move is a large move in returns. Topic 2 Increasing renewable penetration in the NEM

Increasing renewable penetration in the NEM

Storage is critical — and the volume you need climbs steeply, not linearly, as wind and solar head toward 100%. The NEM is already ~44% renewable (grid wind+solar ~29%, plus hydro and rooftop) and spilling 7+ TWh/yr. Play the calculator first: set a wind + solar target, find the mix and the gas balance that keep storage sane.

Calculator · How much storage as wind + solar rise?

Set a wind + solar target, pick the split and the overbuild; the model firms it against a simulated NEM year and reports the 4-hour “big batteries” needed. The point: storage climbs far faster than linearly near 100% — and a little firm gas tames it.

Model & assumptions
A deterministic simulated NEM year at hourly resolution (~184 TWh demand, ~21 GW average / ~28 GW peak, dual summer/winter peaks; solar as a daily bell with seasonal swing, ~32% capacity factor; wind auto-correlated at ~34% CF with four multi-day lulls seeded in). These are potential (unconstrained) CFs — realised NEM CFs today are lower (wind 28%, solar 15%) because of curtailment and older plant. Wind+solar is built to overbuild × target of annual demand; the remaining (1 − target) is firm gas (standing in for today's coal + gas + hydro). A 4-hour battery fleet (power = energy ÷ 4) charges on surplus and discharges on deficit to time-shift renewable energy into the gaps; surplus beyond storage is spilled. The tool finds the minimum storage that lets delivered renewables reach the target — i.e. holds gas to its (1 − target) energy budget. As the target nears 100% that budget → 0, so storage must bridge multi-day lulls a 4-hour battery can't hold, and the model flags it. “Big battery” = 500 MW / 2 GWh. CFs are consistent with the AEMO-derived Lens figures; a stylised teaching model for the shape of the answer, not a capacity-expansion study.

◆ Context — who sets the price (secondary)

A decade of NEMDE price-setting attribution — how often each fuel set the 5-minute price. It's a secondary story here, but it's the flip side of the calculator: as storage does more of the firming, it also increasingly sets the price.

Share of 5-min energy price-setting intervals by fuel — % (2015 → 2026 YTD)
Reading the trend
  • Storage set the price 0.1% of intervals in 2015; in 2026 it's 31.5% — as often as all fossil generation combined, which fell from 78% to 31%.
  • As coal exits, the marginal price is increasingly whatever a battery will charge or discharge at — so the arbitrage spread becomes the price signal that funds (or starves) the last mile of storage.
Topic 3 Through the lens of demand

Demand has been flat for a decade — could it double?

Operational demand — the electricity the grid must serve, after rooftop solar is netted off behind the meter — has barely moved from ~177 TWh since 2020. But that flatline hides real growth: rooftop quietly added ~16 TWh of behind-the-meter supply (2020→2025), soaking up underlying growth of ~7% so the grid never saw it. That truce is ending — EVs, electrification and data centres pull up. Whether demand grows ~60% or roughly doubles by 2050 is a range of outcomes, not a certainty. Shape matters as much as size; the calculator below builds the duck curve live.

◆ The trend — flat for a decade, then the climb

NEM operational demand sat flat near 177 TWh for years; the ISP 2026 central case now bends up ~60% by 2050. Solid line = actual, dashed = Step Change forecast.

NEM operational demand — TWh/yr (actual 2020–25 · Step Change forecast to 2050)
Reading the trend
  • Operational demand was flat (~177–181 TWh) from 2020 to 2025 — but behind-the-meter rooftop grew from 13 → 29 TWh (+16 TWh) and efficiency did the rest, so underlying use actually rose ~7% while the grid saw none of it.
  • Step Change lifts it to ~290 TWh by 2050 (+62%); the scenario fan runs 244–433 TWh — i.e. anywhere from +38% to ~2.4×. The flat line is ending; the whole game is the slope.
  • The open question: can rooftop keep adding ~2.7 GW/yr fast enough to keep masking the new load? Rooftop has added ~3 TWh/yr; EVs + electrification alone add ~4–5 TWh/yr through the 2030s — so the masking effect starts to lose the race.

◆ What demand does to price — a hand-off to the Market-cycles team

Demand growth isn't just a volume story — it moves the whole price curve, which is exactly what Topic 1 (Market cycles) trades on. Here's the rule of thumb, straight from the data.

Rule of thumb · demand → price (all else equal)

In the normal range, each +1 GW of average demand ≈ +$20/MWh. But the relationship is convex — into scarcity, every extra GW costs far more. NSW daily average, 2025–26:

~6 GW$64/MWh
~7 GW$83/MWh
~8 GW$105/MWh
~9 GW$246/MWh

So EV, electrification and data-centre load that lift average demand a few GW push the pool price up ~$20–40/MWh — and via the returns-leverage panel in Topic 1, that's several points of project IRR. Put simply: demand growth is a tailwind to the price cycle; rooftop growth is the headwind. Which one wins, and when, is the question to hand back to the room.

Topic 4 The economics of building

The more you build, the less it's worth — until coal leaves

Every megawatt of solar pushes its own midday price toward zero — solar's captured value fell from a 2019 premium (1.06× the pool) to ~0.4×, and it did this to itself. But value cycles with the market: scarcity lifts it, oversupply crushes it — and it comes back as coal exits and evenings tighten. Storage is the mirror image: it monetises the spread solar destroys. Start with the trend below.

◆ The trend — cannibalisation, and how it cycles

The heart of this module: value factor = the price a generator actually captures ÷ the pool average. As solar built out it drove down its own midday price — but the value cycles, and as coal exits, the evening scarcity that pays renewables returns. VIC, generation-weighted (2026 is YTD and winter-weighted, so its uptick overstates the annual).

Solar & wind value factor — capture price ÷ pool (VIC, 2019 → 2026 YTD · 1.0× = captures the pool average)
Reading the trend — the night is darkest before the dawn
  • In 2019 solar captured 1.06× the pool — a premium, because it was scarce. As the fleet built out it cannibalised its own midday price: value factor collapsed to ~0.4× by 2021.
  • But it cycles. The 2022 gas shock lifted all hours, so cannibalisation eased (solar back to 0.57×); as the system returned to well-supplied it bit again (0.37× in 2023–24).
  • The dawn: cannibalisation is worst while coal still floods the midday and leaves evenings cheap. As coal exits (Yallourn 2028, Eraring 2029…) evening scarcity returns — and renewables that can time-shift into it (storage, or being firmed) recapture the value. The trough is a phase, not the destination — which is exactly the build-timing question.
◆ The battery mirror — spread, not value factor

Value factor is a generation idea; it doesn't fit a battery. A battery's economics run on the price spread it arbitrages — buy the cheap trough, sell the expensive peak. And that spread behaves like the mirror image of solar's: as the fleet grows it compresses, but it still cycles.

Battery arbitrage spread vs fleet size — top-2hr spread $/MWh (left) & battery count (right), monthly
Reading the trend — the scissors, and the cycle
  • The top-2-hour arbitrage spread fell from ~$500–880/MWh (mid-2024, a handful of batteries) to ~$100–200 by 2026 as the fleet grew to ~60 batteries. More storage competing to buy the trough and sell the peak flattens the very spread it lives on — the mirror image of solar cannibalising its own midday.
  • But it's not all batteries. Spreads also compress when coal baseload is still on the system, in benign weather, and when unit availability is high — and they blow wide in scarcity (note the Jun-2025 spike to ~$1,100 and Jan-2026 to ~$480). So, like value factor, the spread cycles with the market, it doesn't just decay.
  • The fleet, in and coming: ~65 batteries / 12.3 GW / 24 GWh operating today, with ~8 GW / 21 GWh committed and building. Every one that arrives competes for the same spread, so the business case is a moving target — best while you're early, and best where the cycle still throws volatility.

Presenter notes
The uncomfortable truth
This is the topic closest to home for a development team: the projects we build make the next project worse. Don't soften it — sit in it.
What a good answer looks like
A table that connects the physics (solar all generates at once) to the market (self-cannibalising price) to the strategy (firming, storage, or being early). The winning insight is usually “value has moved from energy to flexibility”.
The provocation
“Would you build another unfirmed solar farm in this market?” Force a yes/no from each person, then unpack the disagreement.
The dawn nuance
Don't let the room conclude “renewables can't make money”. The value-factor cycle is the sophisticated read: the trough is a well-supplied-with-coal phase; coal exit + storage that shifts solar into the evening is what recaptures value. Timing and firming, not just penetration.
Watch-out
Battery earnings are the historical spread — as more batteries arrive, that spread compresses too. Nothing is a free lunch forever; make them say what erodes the battery case.
◆ Context — a revenue stream already competed to zero (secondary)

FCAS is the cautionary companion to cannibalisation: the frequency-control revenue that used to underwrite storage fell ~90–95% in five years as batteries flooded in. Energy-arbitrage value can go the same way.

Total FCAS price by state — $/MW/hr (2020 → 2026 YTD)
Reading the trend
  • SA FCAS fell $160 → $6/MW/hr (2020→2026); NSW $69 → $6 — a ~90–95% collapse as storage entered. Only TAS — thin and battery-light — still holds a premium.
  • The lesson for the battery case: a revenue stream can be competed to near-zero faster than a 15-year model assumes. Which layer of the stack survives to year 10?
Topic 5 Award vs concrete vs electrons

Most of the pipeline is a mirage

A vast gap sits between what's proposed, committed, and actually in dispatch. ~177 GW proposed vs ~12 GW committed — a 14× funnel — and transmission, connection and system-strength gate every step. Knowing which signals predict delivery is the whole game.

◆ The trend — the mirage growing

Every monthly AEMO snapshot since 2020: proposed capacity has multiplied ~7× to 346 GW, while committed capacity barely moved and what's actually built crawls. The gap is the mirage.

NEM pipeline by tier — GW (AEMO GI snapshots, 2020 → 2026)
Reading the trend
  • Proposed GW: 53 (2020) → 346 (2026). Committed: 5.8 → 12.8. Built (existing): 52 → 70.
  • The top line's slope is developer optimism; the bottom two are physics, capital and connection. The widening gap is what the back-test cashes out — only ~9% of 2022's proposals are built today.

A table that names the real gates — connection/system strength, transmission, offtake, planning — and can rank which one kills the most megawatts. Bonus: an early signal that actually predicts delivery.

This is pipeline hygiene: the difference between a portfolio of announcements and a portfolio of assets. Tie it to how the team screens opportunities.
