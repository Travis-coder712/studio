So What?

Now that we know how the market works — what do we do differently? A facilitator's guide.

Yesterday we read the NEM. Today is the “so what” — turning an appreciation of how the market is structured into things we start and stop doing. You'll sit in your function, not a project team, and leave with owned changes — not a wall of sticky notes.

The challenge that runs underneath the whole session

If we're to be the best development team in the country — what should we start and stop?

Hold that standard against everything. And its sharper, inverted twin: what would guarantee we're not the best — and are we doing any of it?

The rule for every idea

Make it a concrete Start or a Stop — and lead with stops

Two things only: it's a specific Start or Stop (not “think about”), and, where you can, name what a new start displaces (attention is scarce). It can spring from the day's learning or from your own read of what matters — you don't have to tie it back to a particular insight. Lead with the stops; that's where the capacity comes from.

Seating
Functional teams — Development, Analysis, Land & Planning, Structured Bids, Commercial, and Technical (Engineering). The changes are functional; the function owns them. (Cross-cutting themes — like ways of working — can be raised by any table.)
Materials
A wall or whiteboard per function, cards + markers, dot stickers for voting, and one shared written doc for the commitments (open it on screen — we capture live, we don't photograph a wall).
Output
A short written decision log: for each committed change — the start/stop, the function, what it links to, the trade, an owner, a 30-day first step, and how we'll know it worked. Reviewed at 90 days.

The frame for the whole day:

For every project we're balancing the asset's fundamentals against a few forces — and the one we spent yesterday reading is the market.

Day 1 was our deep look at that force — what the market rewards, and when. Today's job is to let that read re-rank, re-time and re-screen what we develop and how. Policy is a real force too; capital is a background factor — yes, we can't do everything at once, but that's not what today is about. The question: what changes in how we work now that we've read the market?

The asset · and the forces that shape how we progress it

The asset is the object of the decision. A few forces shape how hard and fast we push it — and the market is the one we spent yesterday reading.

The asset

Fundamentals & relative quality

Maturity, critical path, risk, pace, knowns/unknowns — and how it ranks against our other projects and anything we might buy.

“Is this genuinely good — and better than our next option?”
Mostly in our control
Force · Market ★ yesterday's focus

When & what the market rewards

The cycle, cannibalisation, firming, demand, the forward curve. It shapes which projects, which technologies, in which locations, at what point in the cycle — and it's the force we spent Day 1 learning to read.

“Does the market reward this project — and when?”
Not in our control — but now knowable
Force · Policy

Support mechanisms & windows

CIS/LTESA and government support — a floor and a gate. But a contract is not a built asset, and the windows open and shut.

“Is there a mechanism that de-risks it — and is the window open?”
Partly in our control (we bid, we time)
Factor · Capital & the firm

A real constraint — but a background one today

Capital and attention are finite, so you can't do everything at once. Worth naming when two changes compete — but it's not the focus of this session.

“When something has to give, what gives?”
Noted, held lightly
The horizon caveat — read this before you “react” to the market

Development cycles are longer than market and policy cycles

A project takes years; the market and the support settings will turn several times before it reaches COD. So read today's market — but develop to a longer-term view of where market and company need are heading. Informed by the current setting, influenced by it, but not captive to it. The “so what” is a sharper read, not a lurch to chase this year's price.

A light discipline, not a capital exercise

Every “start” still needs a “stop”

Not because of a budget model — because attention is the real scarce resource. If a table can't name what a new “start” displaces, it's a wish, not a priority. Lead with the stops.

Each functional table gets a card with the questions that matter most for your function. Wrestle with them, add your own, and land on concrete starts and stops. You'll notice communication and clarity of decisions recur across several functions — that's the Ways of working strand; flag those for the room.

Thinking tools to break the room out of “add more, defend everything.” Use them to pressure-test candidates and to run the deep-dive.

Default-buster

Stop-first / subtraction

People reflexively add; we systematically miss subtractive fixes. Run the room stop-heavy — the capital and attention for any “start” has to come from a “stop.”

Prompt: “What are we doing out of habit that no longer earns its place?”
For “best in the country”

Inversion

Don't ask how to be the best. Ask: “what would guarantee we're not the best?” List it — then stop doing it. It's faster and more honest.

Munger: “Invert, always invert.”
Deep-dive lens

Pre-mortem

“It's 2028 and this decision failed. Write the story of why.” Surfaces risks that optimism hides, before you commit.

Klein. Ten minutes, silent writing first, then share.
Outside view

The competitor / the acquirer

“If a sharp competitor — or a PE buyer — looked at our portfolio, which project would they cut, and which would they buy?” Kills internal sentiment fast.

Great for portfolio and M&A honesty.
The discipline

The trade (opportunity cost)

For every “start,” force the sentence: “to do this, we stop ___.” If nothing can be named, it's not really a priority — it's a wish.

Ties straight to the capital ceiling.
Triage

Type 1 vs Type 2 decisions

Reversible (Type 2): just try it, don't spend workshop air. Irreversible (Type 1): that's what the room is for. Sort candidates before you deep-dive.

Bezos. Most “changes” are cheap and reversible — just start.
Risk map

Knowns & unknowns

For a project: known-knowns (manage), known-unknowns (investigate now), unknown-unknowns (build slack for). Most critical-path pain lives in the second box, ignored.

Use on the deep-dive project's fundamentals.
Reframe

Work backwards from the market

Not “here's our project, let's justify it.” Start from what the market and policy reward — then ask whether the project fits, and if not, what would.

The Day-1 insights are the starting point, not an afterthought.

A candidate change that deserves its own strand — and a live example of the whole “so what.” It isn't about the market; it's about us, and it's squarely in our control.

The uncomfortable truth

We share knowledge by talking at people — and the durable record is thin

Big meetings with “a million people online”, one person presenting, are low-bandwidth and leave almost no trace. When someone picks up a project later, the critical context lives in a few people's heads and a scatter of emails. If the best team in the country runs on that — they don't.

The standard I'd put on the table
Written-first
Decisions and context get written down, crisply, before the meeting — not after, if ever. A one-page project brief and a running decision log beat a 40-person call. (Amazon's six-pager; GitLab's “handbook-first”.)
The passport
Every project carries a living “project passport” — a structured, current record of fundamentals, critical path, risks, decisions and their why. The test: “if the lead left tomorrow, could someone pick this up from the written record alone?”
AI removes the excuse
The cost of good documentation just collapsed. Capturing a meeting, structuring notes, and writing them with clarity a stranger can use is now near-free. Thin records are no longer a constraint — they're a choice.
Async by default
Reserve live time for genuine debate and decision. Everything one-directional (updates, FYIs, status) goes to writing people read on their own clock — higher bandwidth, and it's durable.
What “effective communication” looks like

Clear · captured · findable · reusable

A decision that a newcomer can understand in five minutes; captured the moment it's made; findable without asking a person; and reusable by whoever inherits the project. If a piece of communication isn't all four, it's theatre.

Meta: this workshop practises what it preaches — the output is a clean written decision log, built live and AI-assisted, not a photo of a whiteboard.
