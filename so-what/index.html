So What?

Now that we know how the market works — what do we do differently? A facilitator's guide.

Yesterday we read the NEM. Today is the “so what” — turning an appreciation of how the market is structured into things we start and stop doing. You'll sit in your function, not a project team, and leave with owned changes — not a wall of sticky notes.

The challenge that runs underneath the whole session

If we're to be the best development team in the country — what should we start and stop?

Hold that standard against everything. And its sharper, inverted twin: what would guarantee we're not the best — and are we doing any of it?

The mechanic — Stop · Start · Continue

Each person brings three; the group backs the best; you make one real

We've got the resources, so this isn't only about cutting. Each person names one thing to Stop, one to Start, and one to Continue. Own ideas are preferred over the prompts. The group votes the best two in each category, reports them, then picks one Start to actually turn into a plan. Keep each one concrete — not “think about”.

Groups
Functional teams — max 5 per group. Where a function has more than five people, split into several groups of ≤5 — each runs the mechanic and reports back separately. Cross-cutting themes (like ways of working) can be raised by any group.
Capture
Two levels. Each person captures their own Stop / Start / Continue; each group captures its voted best-two and the one Start it will make real. Written down, on screen, live — templates are on the “Capture & commit” tab.
Output
Per group: the voted best two in each category, and one Start made real — with an owner and a 30-day first step. Reviewed at 90 days.

The frame for the whole day:

For every project we're balancing the asset's fundamentals against a few forces — and the one we spent yesterday reading is the market.

Day 1 was our deep look at that force — what the market rewards, and when. Today's job is to let that read re-rank, re-time and re-screen what we develop and how. Policy is a real force too; capital is a background factor — yes, we can't do everything at once, but that's not what today is about. The question: what changes in how we work now that we've read the market?

Read this before you “react” to the market

Development cycles are longer than market and policy cycles

A project takes years; the market and the support settings will turn several times before it reaches COD. So read today's market — but develop to a longer-term view of where market and company need are heading. Informed by the current setting, influenced by it, but not captive to it. The “so what” is a sharper read, not a lurch to chase this year's price.

The asset · and the forces that shape how we progress it

The asset is the object of the decision. A few forces shape how hard and fast we push it — and the market is the one we spent yesterday reading.

The asset

Fundamentals & relative quality

Maturity, critical path, risk, pace, knowns/unknowns — and how it ranks against our other projects and anything we might buy.

“Is this genuinely good — and better than our next option?”
Mostly in our control
Force · Market ★ yesterday's focus

When & what the market rewards

The cycle, cannibalisation, firming, demand, the forward curve. It shapes which projects, which technologies, in which locations, at what point in the cycle — and it's the force we spent Day 1 learning to read.

“Does the market reward this project — and when?”
Not in our control — but now knowable
Force · Policy

Support mechanisms & windows

CIS/LTESA and government support — a floor and a gate. But a contract is not a built asset, and the windows open and shut.

“Is there a mechanism that de-risks it — and is the window open?”
Partly in our control (we bid, we time)

A real constraint — but a background one today

Capital and attention are finite, so you can't do everything at once. Worth naming when two changes compete — but it's not the focus of this session.

“When something has to give, what gives?”

Five mental models from the business and investing world to help you spot the areas of opportunity — what to Stop, Start or Continue. You don't need all five: find the one that clicks for you and use it. Open each for how to apply it.

Jeff Bezos · Amazon

Type 1 vs Type 2 decisions

Is this a one-way door (irreversible, Type 1) or a two-way door (reversible, Type 2)? Move fast on the reversible; reserve real deliberation for the few that can't be undone.

How to use it
Most changes you'll debate today are two-way doors — so don't debate, just try them and learn. If a Start is cheap and reversible, that alone is a reason to do it now. Save the caution for the genuine one-way doors (a divestment, a big irreversible commit).
Donald Rumsfeld · NASA

Knowns & unknowns

Sort what you're dealing with into known-knowns (manage), known-unknowns (investigate now), and unknown-unknowns (build slack for). Most critical-path pain hides in the middle box.

How to use it
For a project or a decision, list each. The known-unknowns are gold — each one is often a "Start: go and find out X" before it becomes a nasty surprise. The unknown-unknowns are why you keep some slack in the plan.
Charlie Munger · Berkshire

Inversion

“Invert, always invert.” Instead of “how do we win?”, ask “what would guarantee we lose?” — then stop doing that. Solving the negative is often clearer and faster.

How to use it
The engine for Stops. Ask: “what would guarantee we're not the best development team in the country?” Every honest answer is a candidate Stop. It sidesteps our tendency to defend what we already do.
Howard Marks · Oaktree

Second-order thinking

Don't stop at the obvious first consequence — ask “and then what?” The consequences of the consequences are where the real edge (or the trap) sits.

How to use it
A change that looks good first-order can be poor second-order, and vice versa. “If we do X, then what? And then what?” Two or three steps out. It's how you avoid the obvious move everyone else is also making.
Elon Musk · Aristotle

First principles

Strip a problem back to what's fundamentally true and reason up from there — rather than by analogy (“we've always done it this way”).

How to use it
Great for challenging habits. “Why do we do this — what's the actual requirement underneath?” Often the habit is a leftover from an old constraint that no longer holds — a clean candidate Stop, or a much simpler Start.

A cross-cutting strand any group can pick up — and a live example of the whole “so what.” It isn't about the market; it's about how we work, and it's squarely in our control.

The opportunity — start here

Capture, structure and clarity are near-free now

What used to be expensive — writing something up clearly, structuring a decision, keeping a durable record — AI now does in seconds. We don't want to be swamped by AI text; we want to use that capability to improve our record-keeping and the rationale behind our decisions. So the question for your group: what does that mean for what we should be doing?

Some starting thoughts (bring your own)
Written-first
The crisp record used to cost real effort, so we defaulted to talking at people in big meetings that leave almost no trace. Now a clear one-pager and a running decision log are cheap — and they're durable.
The passport
A living, one-place record per project so anyone can pick it up — see the template below. The test: “if the lead left tomorrow, could someone run this from the written record alone?”
The rationale
Capture the why, not just the what — the decision and the reasoning, so future-us (or a successor) isn't reverse-engineering it from a scatter of emails.
Effective comms
Clear · captured · findable · reusable. Understandable in five minutes, captured the moment it's made, findable without asking a person, reusable by whoever inherits it. If it isn't all four, it's theatre.
Example — the “project passport”

One place per project, with a short summary and current approach/status from each function, plus the decisions and their why. AI can keep it current from meeting notes — so it's a live record, not a chore. A template your teams could adopt:

Project Passport — [Project name]
Region · Technology · Capacity (MW / MWh) · Stage & tier · Project owner · Last updated (auto)
Development
Summary — what it is, where it's up to, the plan. Approach/status — current tier, critical path, next gate, pace vs plan.
Analysis
Summary — the fundamentals (resource, scale, competitiveness). Approach/status — the market read, revenue basis, how it's tiered and benchmarked vs our operating assets.
Land & Planning
Summary — site and land status. Approach/status — planning & approvals, connection and system-strength position, key risks.
Structured Bids
Summary — scheme eligibility and fit. Approach/status — bids submitted/planned, awards and obligations, the next window.
Commercial
Summary — the commercial pathway. Approach/status — offtake / PPA position, the basis of key decisions, and the live options vs the decisions already taken.
Technical (Eng.)
Summary — technology and design choices. Approach/status — technical risks, and resourcing needs & timing.
Decisions log
Decision · date · rationale (the why) · who. The context a newcomer can't get anywhere else.
Open questions
The known-unknowns — what we know we don't know, and who's chasing each.
